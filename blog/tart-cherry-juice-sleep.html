Tart Cherry Juice for Sleep: What the Evidence Actually Shows

August 5, 2026 · 7 min read
Share

Most natural sleep aids trade on tradition rather than trial data. Tart cherry juice is an exception. Montmorency tart cherries (Prunus cerasus) contain one of the highest naturally occurring concentrations of melatonin found in food, along with anthocyanins that appear to slow the enzyme responsible for breaking that melatonin down. The result is a small but genuine body of randomized controlled trial evidence — not just observational correlation.

The Key Studies

A 2012 crossover trial published in the European Journal of Nutrition gave healthy adults 30ml of concentrated Montmorency tart cherry juice twice daily for seven days. Compared to placebo, participants showed significantly increased urinary melatonin levels, longer time in bed, longer total sleep time, and higher sleep efficiency measured by actigraphy.

A separate 2010 pilot study in the Journal of Medical Food, led by sleep researcher Wilfred Pigeon at the University of Rochester, tested tart cherry juice specifically in adults with chronic insomnia. Two weeks of supplementation modestly reduced insomnia severity index scores and increased sleep time by roughly 84 minutes on average — a meaningful effect size for a food-based intervention, though the sample size was small (n=15) and the study lacked a placebo arm.

Why It Works — Two Mechanisms

The melatonin content is the more obvious driver: tart cherries are one of the few plant sources with quantifiable melatonin, though the amount varies significantly by cultivar and processing method. The second mechanism is less discussed — tart cherry anthocyanins inhibit indoleamine 2,3-dioxygenase, an enzyme that diverts tryptophan away from the serotonin/melatonin synthesis pathway. In effect, tart cherry juice may help the body both supply melatonin directly and produce more of its own.

There's a secondary anti-inflammatory pathway worth noting. Tart cherries reduce circulating levels of C-reactive protein and other inflammatory markers in several exercise-recovery studies. Since systemic inflammation is independently linked to fragmented sleep, some of the sleep benefit may be indirect.

Dose and Timing

The trial-tested protocol is roughly 30ml (about 2 tablespoons) of concentrated Montmorency tart cherry juice, taken twice daily — once in the morning, once one to two hours before bed. If using whole juice rather than concentrate, the equivalent volume is closer to 240ml twice daily, since it's diluted. Capsule and gummy forms standardize dosing more precisely but vary in extract ratio, so check the label for the equivalent cherry content rather than just the capsule weight. A concentrated organic juice gives the most direct dose control; a gummy format is easier to travel with and dose consistently.

What It Won't Do

Tart cherry juice is not a substitute for treating an underlying sleep disorder. The effect sizes in the trials above are real but modest — think "falls asleep somewhat faster, sleeps somewhat longer," not "cures insomnia." It's also not risk-free: the juice is high in natural sugars, and people managing blood glucose should favor capsule or unsweetened concentrate forms and account for the carbohydrate load. Anyone on blood thinners should check with a physician, since high-anthocyanin foods can have mild antiplatelet effects.

Who It's Worth Trying For

The best-supported use case is otherwise-healthy adults with mild sleep-onset difficulty or short total sleep time who want a food-based intervention before reaching for supplements with stronger central nervous system effects. It pairs reasonably with sleep hygiene basics — consistent wake time, evening light reduction, and a cool bedroom — rather than replacing them. Given the seven-to-fourteen-day windows used in the actual trials, a fair test is at least two weeks of consistent twice-daily use before judging whether it's doing anything for you.
