Vitamin B6 (Pyridoxine): Benefits, Dosage & Deficiency Guide

September 16, 2026 · 8 min read
Share

Vitamin B6 is a cofactor in more than 100 enzymatic reactions, more than almost any other vitamin, spanning amino acid metabolism, neurotransmitter synthesis, and red blood cell formation. It exists in the diet and in supplements as several related compounds — pyridoxine, pyridoxal, and pyridoxamine — which the liver converts into the active coenzyme form, pyridoxal 5'-phosphate (PLP). Despite its central metabolic role, B6 rarely gets discussed with the same attention as B12 or folate, partly because outright deficiency is uncommon in people eating a varied diet — but marginal insufficiency, and separately, toxicity from long-term high-dose supplementation, are both real and underappreciated.

What It Actually Does

PLP is required for the synthesis of serotonin, dopamine, GABA, and norepinephrine — the core neurotransmitters implicated in mood, motivation, and stress regulation. It's also essential for converting the amino acid homocysteine into cysteine, working alongside folate and B12 in that pathway; elevated homocysteine is an independent risk marker for cardiovascular disease, which is why B6 status is sometimes measured alongside those other B vitamins in cardiovascular risk workups. Additionally, B6 is required for hemoglobin synthesis, meaning a deficiency can produce a specific type of anemia even when iron levels are normal.

Deficiency Signs

True B6 deficiency is uncommon in developed countries but occurs more frequently in older adults, people with alcohol use disorder, and those with malabsorption conditions like celiac or Crohn's disease. Certain medications also deplete B6, most notably isoniazid (used for tuberculosis) and some long-term users of oral contraceptives or the Parkinson's drug levodopa. Symptoms include a scaly rash around the mouth and eyes (seborrheic dermatitis), cracked lips, a sore or swollen tongue (glossitis), mood changes including irritability and depression, and in more severe or prolonged cases, peripheral neuropathy — numbness or tingling in the hands and feet, which is notable because very high-dose supplementation can paradoxically cause the same symptom.

The Toxicity Paradox

Unlike most water-soluble vitamins, B6 has a well-documented toxicity syndrome at high doses. A body of case reports and studies dating back to Schaumburg and colleagues' 1983 paper in the New England Journal of Medicine established that chronic intake above roughly 1,000mg per day — and in some sensitive individuals, doses as low as 100–200mg daily sustained over months to years — can cause a sensory peripheral neuropathy: numbness, tingling, and in severe cases difficulty walking. This is largely reversible after stopping supplementation, but recovery can take months. The Tolerable Upper Intake Level set by the National Academy of Medicine is 100mg/day for adults. Most multivitamins and B-complex products stay well under this, but standalone high-dose B6 products (some marketed at 100mg or more per tablet) warrant caution with long-term daily use.

Dosage

The Recommended Dietary Allowance is modest: 1.3mg/day for most adults under 50, rising to 1.5mg (women) or 1.7mg (men) after age 50, when absorption efficiency declines. Pregnant and breastfeeding women need slightly more, around 1.9–2.0mg/day. These amounts are easily obtained from a normal diet — chickpeas, poultry, fish, potatoes, and fortified cereals are all good sources — which is why routine high-dose supplementation isn't necessary for most healthy adults. Clinical use of higher doses (25–100mg) is more targeted, such as for nausea in pregnancy, where a combination of B6 and doxylamine is an established first-line treatment recommended by the American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists.

Form Matters: Pyridoxine vs. P5P

Most over-the-counter B6 supplements use pyridoxine hydrochloride, the synthetic, stable form that the liver converts to active PLP. A smaller category of products supply pyridoxal 5'-phosphate (P5P) directly — the already-active coenzyme form. In theory, P5P bypasses a conversion step, which may matter for people with impaired liver conversion capacity, but for most healthy adults, the clinical difference between the two forms in typical supplemental doses hasn't been convincingly demonstrated in controlled trials. Thorne's Pyridoxal 5'-Phosphate is a well-tested option for readers who specifically want the active form, while standard pyridoxine products remain the more widely studied and less expensive default.

If you're taking a B-complex or multivitamin already, check the label before adding standalone B6 — it's easy to unintentionally exceed sensible long-term intake by stacking multiple products, and the deficiency and toxicity symptoms overlap in ways that make self-diagnosis unreliable. A basic blood panel measuring plasma PLP can confirm actual status rather than guessing from symptoms alone.
