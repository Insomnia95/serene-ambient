Body Doubling: Why Working Alongside Someone Boosts Focus

August 29, 2026 · 7 min read
Share

Body doubling means doing a task in the physical or virtual presence of another person who isn't necessarily helping you — they're just there, working on their own thing, while you work on yours. It's become one of the most widely recommended strategies in ADHD communities for tackling procrastination on unpleasant or effortful tasks, and platforms built entirely around the concept — co-working video calls, silent study rooms, paid accountability sessions — have grown fast over the past several years. The honest starting point is that the evidence base is thinner than the popularity suggests.

What's Actually Been Studied

A 2024 qualitative study in ACM Transactions on Accessible Computing interviewed neurodivergent adults about their body doubling practices and found consistent self-reported benefits: participants described feeling more able to initiate tasks, less prone to drifting into distraction, and more comfortable staying accountable to a task when someone else's presence was involved — even when that person offered no direct help. But the same body of research is explicit about its limits: there have been no controlled experiments directly testing body doubling, and the mechanisms behind why it seems to work remain unconfirmed. What exists is self-report and qualitative interview data, not randomized trials.

The Likely Mechanism: Social Facilitation

Body doubling almost certainly draws on a much older and better-established phenomenon: social facilitation, first formalized by psychologist Robert Zajonc in 1965. Zajonc's drive theory held that the mere presence of others increases physiological arousal, which improves performance on simple or well-practiced tasks but can impair performance on complex or unfamiliar ones. This is a plausible partial explanation for why body doubling seems to help with tasks people already know how to do but are avoiding — paperwork, cleaning, routine work — more than it helps with genuinely difficult, novel problem-solving. It also overlaps with the Hawthorne effect, where simply being observed changes behavior, and with basic commitment-device logic: knowing someone else is aware of what you're supposed to be doing raises the social cost of not doing it.

Who Reports the Most Benefit

Clinical write-ups from institutions like the Cleveland Clinic frame body doubling primarily as an ADHD executive-function support — useful for task initiation, the specific place where many people with ADHD get stuck, since starting a task requires a burst of executive control that's disproportionately hard to summon alone. But the technique isn't ADHD-specific in practice. Chronic procrastinators, remote workers without built-in social structure, and anyone doing solitary creative or administrative work report similar effects. It's worth noting the same sources flag that body doubling isn't universal: for some people, another person's presence is itself a distraction, or turns a task into a performance rather than a focus aid — the opposite of the intended effect.

Making It Work

The setup that shows up most consistently in both clinical guidance and user reports: the other person doesn't need to be doing the same task, or even a related one — parallel, independent work is enough. Video-call co-working and in-person study spaces both get reported as effective, suggesting the presence itself matters more than physical proximity. Removing outside noise so the "presence" signal stays clean also comes up often — reusable earplugs for shared spaces, or noise-isolating headphones for video co-working sessions where you want to hear a call partner but block a noisy room.

Pairing body doubling with a visible time structure — a session that has a defined start and end — seems to compound the accountability effect, since it converts an open-ended task into a bounded commitment you've made in front of someone else. A visual countdown timer on the desk during a co-working session gives both people a shared, non-verbal cue for when the block starts and ends.

Bottom Line

Body doubling is a low-cost, low-risk technique with strong anecdotal and self-reported support, particularly for task initiation in ADHD and chronic procrastination. It has not been tested in controlled trials, and its mechanism is best explained by borrowing from older, better-validated research on social facilitation rather than anything unique to the practice itself. That's not a reason to dismiss it — plenty of useful tools predate their formal evidence base — but it is a reason to treat claims about exactly how or why it works with some caution, and to notice honestly whether it's helping you or just adding social pressure without the focus payoff.
