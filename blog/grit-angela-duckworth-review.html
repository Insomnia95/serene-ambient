Grit by Angela Duckworth: What the Research Says About Passion and Perseverance

September 25, 2026 · 9 min read
Share

Grit made a simple, appealing claim on the way to becoming a bestseller: passion and sustained perseverance toward long-term goals predict achievement better than raw talent does. Angela Duckworth, a psychologist who left a management consulting career to teach middle school math before moving into academic research, built the book around her own studies on West Point cadets, National Spelling Bee finalists, and salespeople. The framework is intuitive and has aged into a fixture of parenting and self-help advice. The harder question is how much of it survived contact with independent replication.

Where the Grit Scale Came From

Duckworth's foundational paper, published with Peterson, Matthews, and Kelly in the Journal of Personality and Social Psychology in 2007, introduced the 12-item Grit Scale, measuring two components: consistency of interests over time and perseverance of effort despite setbacks. In one of the paper's headline findings, grit scores predicted which cadets would survive West Point's grueling summer training program, "Beast Barracks," better than the Whole Candidate Score the academy itself used for admissions — a genuinely striking result, since that score already incorporates SAT scores, class rank, and leadership ratings.

The Spelling Bee and Retention Studies

The same 2007 paper found grit predicted how far contestants advanced in the Scripps National Spelling Bee, and later work by Eskreis-Winkler and colleagues, published in 2014 in Frontiers in Psychology, found grit scores predicted retention among National Guard trainees, sales agents, and high school dropout risk across several independent samples. These studies share a specific strength: they measure a real, hard behavioral outcome (did the person quit or not) rather than a self-reported proxy, which is exactly the kind of criterion validity that survey-based psychology constructs often lack.

Where the Science Gets Thinner

The most consequential pushback came from a 2017 meta-analysis by Credé, Tynan, and Harms in the same journal that published Duckworth's original paper. Pooling 88 independent samples and roughly 66,000 participants, they found grit's overall correlation with performance and retention outcomes was modest, around r=0.18, and that this effect was driven almost entirely by the "perseverance of effort" sub-scale — a construct that overlaps heavily with conscientiousness, one of the Big Five personality traits that had already been well-studied for decades. The "consistency of interests" half of the scale, meanwhile, showed a much weaker relationship to outcomes. In plainer terms: grit as a whole construct predicts less than the book implies, and the part that does predict something is largely a repackaging of a trait psychologists already had a name for.

Duckworth has acknowledged some of this in follow-up writing, noting that grit was never intended to fully replace existing personality frameworks and that its practical value lies partly in how actionable and communicable it is compared to "conscientiousness" as a coaching concept. That's a fair defense of the book's usefulness, but it's a different claim than the one the book's marketing and most popular summaries lead with.

The Deliberate Practice Connection

Duckworth frequently pairs grit with Anders Ericsson's deliberate practice research to explain how sustained effort translates into skill. This pairing is worth scrutinizing on its own terms: a 2014 meta-analysis by Macnamara, Hambrick, and Oswald in Psychological Science found that deliberate practice explained only about 12% of the variance in performance across domains overall, and considerably less in fields like education and professions than in games and music. Peak, Ericsson's own book on the subject, is more careful about these limits than most secondhand summaries of the "10,000-hour rule" tend to be, and reading it alongside Grit clarifies where perseverance research is genuinely strong versus where it's stretched to explain outcomes it wasn't built to explain.

What the Book Gets Right

Setting the effect-size debate aside, Grit's actual behavioral recommendations hold up reasonably well: build a "grit-scale" habit of returning to a goal after setbacks rather than switching goals reactively, pair long-term interest with deliberate skill-building rather than motivation alone, and treat sustained effort as a trainable behavior rather than a fixed trait. That last point connects directly to Carol Dweck's growth mindset research, which Duckworth cites approvingly throughout the book. Mindset makes a more empirically cautious version of a similar argument, and the two books are frequently — and reasonably — read as companions.
