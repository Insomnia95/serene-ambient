Electrolytes: The Overlooked Piece of Hydration

August 10, 2026 · 8 min read
Share

Drink more water is the default advice for almost every symptom of fatigue, headache, or sluggishness. It's incomplete. Plain water dilutes the sodium concentration in your blood, and below a certain threshold, your kidneys respond by excreting the water right back out rather than letting you absorb it. Rehydration isn't just about volume — it's about the mineral gradient that pulls water into your cells in the first place.

Why Water Alone Can Fall Short

A 2016 study in the American Journal of Clinical Nutrition by Maughan et al. introduced the Beverage Hydration Index, comparing how long different drinks are retained in the body after ingestion. Plain water scored near the bottom. Beverages containing sodium — including oral rehydration solutions and milk — scored significantly higher, because sodium slows the rate at which the kidneys clear fluid, keeping more of it circulating rather than passing straight through as urine.

This matters most after sweating. Sweat isn't just water leaving the body — it carries sodium, potassium, and smaller amounts of magnesium and calcium with it. Replacing volume without replacing the minerals lost can leave you drinking your way into a lower blood sodium concentration than before you started.

Hyponatremia: The Overcorrection

The clearest evidence for electrolyte importance comes from a 2005 New England Journal of Medicine study on Boston Marathon runners by Almond et al., which found that 13 percent of finishers had biochemical hyponatremia — abnormally low blood sodium — largely from overdrinking plain water during the race without adequate sodium intake. Some cases were severe enough to require hospitalization. The researchers noted that the drive to "stay hydrated" had, paradoxically, become a health risk when sodium replacement was ignored.

This isn't only a marathon problem. Anyone sweating heavily for an extended period — through heat, exercise, illness, or sauna use — is subject to the same mechanism at a smaller scale, which is why electrolyte-specific products like LMNT electrolyte drink mix exist as a distinct category from flavored water.

Potassium's Separate Role

Sodium gets most of the attention, but potassium does independent work — it's the primary intracellular ion, balancing sodium's extracellular dominance and regulating cell membrane potential, which affects everything from muscle contraction to heart rhythm. A 2013 meta-analysis in the BMJ by Aburto et al., covering data from over 1.28 million person-years, found that higher potassium intake was associated with a 24 percent reduction in stroke risk and measurable reductions in blood pressure, particularly in people with higher sodium intake. Most people undershoot potassium far more than they undershoot sodium — Western diets tend to run sodium-heavy and potassium-light, the inverse of what ancestral diets provided.

Magnesium and Cramping

Magnesium's role in hydration is smaller but real — it's a cofactor in the sodium-potassium pump that moves ions across cell membranes, the same pump responsible for muscle relaxation after contraction. Deficiency is linked to increased cramping susceptibility during endurance exercise, though the research here is less conclusive than for sodium and potassium; a 2020 review in Nutrients noted mixed results across trials, with clearer benefit in people who were magnesium-deficient to begin with rather than in already-replete athletes.

Who Actually Needs Supplemental Electrolytes

Not everyone. If you're sedentary, live in a temperate climate, and eat a varied diet, dietary sodium and potassium from food are typically sufficient. Electrolyte supplementation earns its place under specific conditions: exercise sessions longer than 60–90 minutes, heat exposure, illness involving vomiting or diarrhea, low-carbohydrate diets (which increase sodium excretion through a mechanism tied to lower insulin levels), and morning fatigue that persists despite adequate water intake.

The American College of Sports Medicine's position stand on fluid replacement, updated by Sawka et al. in Medicine & Science in Sports & Exercise, recommends sodium intake during prolonged exercise specifically to offset sweat losses and reduce the risk of both cramping and hyponatremia — not as a blanket recommendation for all hydration.
