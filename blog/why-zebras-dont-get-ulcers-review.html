Why Zebras Don't Get Ulcers: What Sapolsky Got Right About Chronic Stress

July 22, 2026 · 9 min read
Share

A zebra being chased by a lion is under intense, life-threatening stress. Ninety seconds later, the chase is over — the zebra is either dead or grazing again, its stress hormones back to baseline. Humans rarely get that resolution. We activate the same physiological stress response sitting in traffic, rereading an unanswered email, or lying awake at 3 a.m. rehearsing an argument that already happened. Robert Sapolsky's Why Zebras Don't Get Ulcers, now in its third edition, is the most cited popular-science explanation of why this distinction — acute stress versus chronic stress — determines whether stress kills you or just annoys you.

The Core Argument

Sapolsky, a Stanford neuroendocrinologist who has spent decades studying wild baboons in Kenya, builds the book around one mechanism: the stress response evolved to handle short physical emergencies, not prolonged psychological ones. When a zebra sees a lion, cortisol and adrenaline surge, glucose floods into the bloodstream, digestion halts, and the immune system deprioritizes long-term repair in favor of immediate muscle output. All of that is adaptive if the emergency lasts three minutes. It becomes destructive if the body stays in that state for three months.

Humans do exactly that. Sapolsky's research on wild baboon troops found that an animal's rank and social stability predicted its baseline cortisol and cardiovascular risk more reliably than its diet did. The same pattern shows up in humans: the Whitehall studies of British civil servants, which Sapolsky cites extensively, found that lower-rung employees with less control over their workload had significantly higher rates of cardiovascular disease than senior staff — independent of smoking, diet, or exercise.

Why Chronic Stress Makes You Sick

The book walks through specific organ systems. Chronically elevated cortisol suppresses immune function, which is why stressed people catch colds more often and heal slower. It redirects blood glucose for a fight-or-flight response that never comes, contributing to insulin resistance over time. It shrinks dendritic branching in the hippocampus — the brain region responsible for memory and stress regulation — while simultaneously strengthening the amygdala, the fear-processing center. That combination partly explains why chronic stress makes people both more forgetful and more anxious at once.

Sapolsky is careful to distinguish this from fear-mongering about stress in general. Acute, resolved stress — a hard workout, a tight deadline you actually hit, a scary movie — isn't harmful and can even improve resilience through a process called hormesis. The damage comes specifically from stress without resolution: the kind with no clear endpoint, no sense of control, and no outlet for the physical energy the stress response mobilizes.

The Predictability and Control Variables

Two factors determine how damaging a stressor is, according to the research Sapolsky reviews: predictability and perceived control. Animals — and people — subjected to unpredictable stressors show far worse physiological outcomes than those facing the same objective stressor on a predictable schedule. Similarly, having any sense of control, even illusory control, measurably blunts the cortisol response. This is a large part of why open-plan offices with unpredictable interruptions and no autonomy over one's schedule are so reliably associated with burnout in later research building on Sapolsky's framework.

For readers who want the deeper neuroscience, Sapolsky's later book Behave extends this into a full account of what drives human behavior at the intersection of biology and circumstance — useful context for anyone who finished Zebras and wants to go further into the mechanisms.

What the Book Doesn't Solve

Sapolsky is a scientist explaining a mechanism, not a self-help author offering a program. The last chapters on coping — exercise, social support, a sense of control, outlets for frustration — are useful but general. If you want to actually track whether your own physiology is running in a chronic stress state, pairing the book's framework with objective data helps. Heart rate variability is the most accessible proxy for autonomic nervous system balance outside a lab, and wearables that track it overnight can show, in real numbers, whether a stretch of bad weeks is actually showing up in your body.

Who Should Read It

Anyone dealing with unresolved, low-grade chronic stress — the kind that doesn't have a single dramatic source but instead comes from an accumulation of unpredictability and low control — will find the book reframes the problem usefully. It's dense in places (Sapolsky doesn't shy away from endocrinology), but the writing is unusually funny for a book about stress hormones. Nearly thirty years after its first edition, it remains the clearest single explanation of why the stress that kills people looks nothing like the stress that killed zebras.
