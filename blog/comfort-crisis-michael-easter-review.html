The Comfort Crisis by Michael Easter: Book Review and Key Takeaways

August 25, 2026 · 9 min read
Share

Michael Easter's 2021 book The Comfort Crisis opens with a premise backed by more than opinion: humans evolved under conditions of scarcity, cold, hunger, and physical strain, and the near-total elimination of those stressors in modern life hasn't made us happier — it's correlated with rising rates of anxiety, depression, and metabolic disease. Easter, a journalism professor at UNLV, spends the book testing that idea directly, including a 33-day backcountry hunting trip in the Alaskan wilderness with no phone, no scale, and minimal food.

The Core Argument

The book's central claim rests on a body of research showing that humans are, evolutionarily, mismatched with constant ease. Easter cites data from the World Health Organization showing depression as a leading cause of disability worldwide, and connects it to studies on "hedonic adaptation" — the finding that comfort, once achieved, stops registering as pleasurable and instead becomes the new baseline. The book isn't anti-technology or anti-modernity; its argument is narrower and better supported than that: specific types of voluntary discomfort — cold, physical exertion, boredom, and scarcity — appear to have measurable effects on mood, focus, and physical resilience.

Misogi: Doing One Hard Thing a Year

One of the book's most repeated ideas is "misogi," a concept Easter borrows from ultrarunner Marcus Elliott: choose one extremely difficult physical or mental challenge per year, with two rules — it should have roughly a 50 percent chance of failure, and it shouldn't be something you can simply buy your way through. The value isn't the specific challenge; it's the psychological carryover. Studies on self-efficacy, going back to Albert Bandura's foundational work in the 1970s and 80s, support the mechanism: succeeding at a difficult, self-chosen task increases confidence in unrelated future challenges.

Rucking and the "Rule of 3s"

Easter dedicates significant space to rucking — walking with a weighted pack — as a low-impact way to build both cardiovascular and muscular fitness simultaneously. A 2018 study cited in the book, conducted with U.S. military populations, found rucking builds strength and endurance with less joint stress than running. He also revisits survival researcher Claude Piantadosi's "Rule of 3s": humans can survive roughly three minutes without air, three days without water, and three weeks without food — a framework Easter uses to argue that most of what we treat as urgent scarcity (a missed meal, a slow WiFi connection) isn't scarcity at all by any evolutionary standard, and that recalibrating that internal alarm reduces everyday anxiety.

Cold, Boredom, and Attention

The book also covers cold exposure research from Wim Hof-adjacent studies and a 2015 study in the journal Temperature on cold's effects on brown fat activation and mood. Perhaps more novel is Easter's chapter on boredom, drawing on attention restoration theory — the same research base behind forest-bathing studies — to argue that constant stimulation from phones has crowded out the "mind-wandering" state associated with creative problem solving and emotional processing.

Where the Book Is Weaker

The Comfort Crisis is a work of journalism, not a clinical trial. Easter is transparent that his personal Alaska trip is anecdote, not data, and the book is strongest when it sticks close to cited research and weaker in its more speculative extrapolations about ancestral lifestyles, an area where evolutionary psychology claims are often difficult to verify. Readers looking for a rigorous meta-analysis should treat this as a well-sourced entry point rather than a definitive text — Easter cites his sources but doesn't always represent the strength or limitations of the underlying studies in detail.

Practical Takeaways

The book's most actionable ideas translate directly into habits: a once-a-year self-chosen physical challenge, regular rucking instead of purely low-effort cardio, scheduled cold exposure, and deliberate unstimulated time without a phone. None of these require the Alaska trip — Easter himself frames the extreme version as illustrative, not prescriptive.
