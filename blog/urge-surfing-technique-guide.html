Urge Surfing: The Neuroscience of Riding Out a Craving

August 28, 2026 · 8 min read
Share

The term "urge surfing" was coined in 1985 by psychologist G. Alan Marlatt as part of relapse prevention therapy for addiction. His observation was simple: people try to resist a craving by fighting it head-on — clenching against it, arguing with it, willing it to disappear — and that resistance is exactly what makes it feel unbearable and often triggers the behavior they're trying to avoid. Marlatt proposed treating the craving instead as a wave. It builds, peaks, and inevitably breaks and dissipates, typically within 15 to 20 minutes, whether or not you act on it. The job isn't to stop the wave. It's to stay on the board.

Why Fighting an Urge Backfires

Thought suppression research consistently shows that trying not to think about something increases its frequency and intensity — the well-documented "ironic process" effect. Cravings behave the same way. When you white-knuckle against an urge to check your phone, eat something, or act on an anxious impulse, you're adding a second layer of distress — the original urge plus the frustration of fighting it — which is why willpower-based resistance is so exhausting and so often fails within minutes.

What the Brain Imaging Shows

A 2013 study published in Social Cognitive and Affective Neuroscience by Westbrook and colleagues put smokers with cravings into an fMRI scanner and had them either react normally or apply mindful, non-judgmental attention to the craving — essentially urge surfing in real time. The mindful-attention condition significantly reduced activity in the subgenual anterior cingulate cortex, a region tied to craving intensity, and lowered self-reported craving, even though participants weren't told to resist or distract themselves. The craving simply had less neural fuel when it was observed instead of fought.

This lines up with work from psychiatrist and neuroscientist Judson Brewer, whose smoking-cessation studies found that a brief mindfulness-based curriculum built around observing cravings without reacting outperformed the American Lung Association's standard treatment — roughly five times the quit rate at six-month follow-up, published in Drug and Alcohol Dependence in 2011. His book The Craving Mind lays out the mechanism: cravings are reinforced by a reward-based learning loop, and curiosity about the physical sensation of the urge interrupts that loop more effectively than suppression does.

The Interoception Piece

Urge surfing works partly because it trains interoceptive awareness — the ability to notice bodily sensations as they arise, largely mediated by the insula. Instead of labeling the experience "anxiety" or "craving" and reacting to the label, you describe the raw sensation: tightness in the chest, heat in the face, a pulling feeling behind the eyes. Sensations described this concretely are easier to tolerate because they're finite and physical rather than abstract and threatening. This is also core CBT territory — Seth Gillihan's Retrain Your Brain walks through the same principle applied to anxious and depressive thought spirals.

How to Do It

When an urge or anxious impulse hits: pause before acting, and locate where in the body the sensation is strongest. Rate its intensity, then describe it in physical terms rather than emotional ones — not "I'm anxious" but "there's a hot, tight feeling in my chest." Breathe normally and keep observing as the sensation shifts, because it will; urges are rarely static, they crest and change shape. Resist the urge to solve it. Watch the wave until it breaks. Most cravings and anxious surges peak and fade within 15 to 20 minutes if no action is taken to prolong them, a timeframe supported by the addiction-craving literature Marlatt's original protocol was built on.

Where It's Most Useful

Urge surfing was developed for substance cravings, but the same mechanism applies to compulsive phone checking, anxious reassurance-seeking, panic-driven avoidance, and stress eating. It is not a substitute for clinical treatment of addiction or an anxiety disorder — it's a skill that sits alongside therapy, not in place of it.
