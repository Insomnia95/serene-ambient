Affect Labeling: Why Naming Your Emotions Calms Your Brain

September 14, 2026 · 7 min read
Share

"Name it to tame it" has become a therapy-office cliché, but the phrase is a fair summary of a real and fairly startling finding in affective neuroscience: putting a feeling into words appears to measurably dampen the brain's threat response to that feeling, in real time, without requiring you to change your circumstances or think your way out of anything. The technique is called affect labeling, and unlike a lot of pop-psychology shortcuts, it has a well-replicated neuroimaging literature behind it.

The Original Finding

The foundational study is a 2007 paper by Matthew Lieberman and colleagues at UCLA, published in Psychological Science under the title "Putting Feelings Into Words." Participants viewed images of faces expressing strong emotions while undergoing fMRI. When they simply looked at the faces, the amygdala — the brain's rapid threat-detection center — lit up as expected. But when participants were asked to select a word describing the emotion shown (angry, afraid) rather than just observing it, amygdala activity dropped, and activity increased in the right ventrolateral prefrontal cortex, a region associated with regulating emotional response. The linguistic act of labeling appeared to recruit a regulatory brain network that quieted the threat-detection network — an effect the researchers termed "incidental emotion regulation," because participants weren't trying to calm down; the calming happened as a byproduct of naming.

Does It Hold Up Outside the Scanner?

A frequent criticism of single-lab fMRI findings is that they don't translate to anything a person can actually use. A 2012 study by Katharina Kircanski, Lieberman, and Michelle Craske, published in Psychological Science, tested affect labeling directly against exposure therapy for spider phobia. Participants approached a live tarantula while either labeling their emotions ("I feel anxious and disgusted by the spider in front of me"), using cognitive reappraisal, distraction, or no strategy at all. Affect labeling produced the largest reduction in skin conductance — a physiological measure of arousal — and predicted the lowest fear responses at a follow-up exposure one week later, outperforming reappraisal and distraction on this measure. That's a meaningful result: it suggests the effect isn't just an artifact of lying still in a brain scanner, and that simply describing what you feel can outperform strategies that require actively reframing the thought.

Why Specificity Seems to Matter

Research on "emotional granularity" — most associated with psychologist Lisa Feldman Barrett — adds a second layer to this picture. People who can distinguish between related-but-distinct emotional states (irritated versus resentful versus disappointed, rather than a blanket "bad") tend to show better emotion regulation and lower rates of problem drinking and other maladaptive coping under stress, based on experience-sampling studies tracking people's emotion words against their coping behaviors over time. The implication for affect labeling is that vague labels ("I feel bad") likely engage the mechanism weakly, while specific labels ("I feel a knot of dread about tomorrow's meeting, separate from being tired") appear to engage it more fully — though this remains an area of active research rather than settled fact.

Labeling Is Not Rumination

It's worth distinguishing affect labeling from its unhelpful cousin. Rumination involves repeatedly analyzing why you feel something, replaying causes, and searching for meaning — a process linked to worsened mood in the depression and anxiety literature. Affect labeling, as tested in these studies, is brief and descriptive: you name the feeling once, without an extended narrative about why you have it or what it says about you. The Lieberman studies used single-word or short-phrase labels, applied in the moment, not a diary entry unpacking the feeling's origin.

How to Use It

The practical version is close to the lab protocol: when you notice anxiety rising, silently or out loud name the specific emotion and, if useful, the sensation ("tight chest, anxious, a little embarrassed"). Some people find it helps to write the label down rather than just think it, which is where a structured emotion journal becomes useful — it forces the specificity that seems to matter, rather than defaulting to "stressed" every time. This pairs naturally with other in-the-moment anxiety tools; if you already use box breathing during acute anxiety, adding a labeling step before or during the breathing exercise costs a few seconds and has independent evidence behind it.

Building the Vocabulary

Because specificity appears to matter, expanding your working emotional vocabulary is itself a useful exercise, not just a therapy-speak nicety. Brené Brown's Atlas of the Heart catalogs and distinguishes 87 discrete emotional states specifically to help readers move past vague descriptors, and Karla McLaren's The Language of Emotions takes a similar granular approach from a counseling background.
