Concurrent Training: How to Combine Strength and Endurance

November 7, 2026 · 9 min read
Share XFacebookPinterest

Many people want two things at once: to become stronger and to improve endurance. Training both qualities in the same week is called concurrent training. The familiar warning is that cardio “kills gains” through an interference effect. That slogan contains a real scientific question, but it exaggerates the practical threat for most recreational trainees.

The relevant question is not whether endurance and strength send perfectly identical signals. They do not. It is whether the combination meaningfully reduces the outcome you care about, given your experience, weekly volume, exercise choices, recovery, and the way sessions are arranged.

What the interference effect actually means

In research, concurrent training is compared with resistance-only or endurance-only training. If the combined group improves less in strength, power, hypertrophy, or aerobic capacity than the specialized group, that gap may be called interference. It does not mean the combined group became weaker or less fit. Often, both qualities still improve.

A 2024 meta-analysis of 59 studies and 1,346 adults found a small reduction in lower-body strength adaptation in men, but not women, with concurrent training. The authors found no sex difference for upper-body strength, power, or VO2max and noted too little evidence for firm conclusions about hypertrophy. Female and highly trained participants remained underrepresented, so the subgroup result is informative rather than a universal biological rule.

Training status changes the stakes

Beginners have wide room to improve. A person starting sensible lifting and aerobic work can often gain strength while building endurance because neither stimulus is near an individual ceiling. More advanced athletes have smaller margins. Their sessions are harder, their goals are more specific, and a modest reduction in adaptation can matter.

A 2021 meta-analysis found that concurrent training reduced lower-body one-repetition-maximum gains in trained participants compared with resistance training alone, but not in moderately trained or untrained participants. The negative effect was more apparent when resistance and endurance occurred in the same session. This is a reason for advanced athletes to plan carefully, not a reason for everyone else to avoid aerobic exercise.

Protect the priority

If strength is the main goal, place the most important strength work when you are fresh. If race preparation is the priority, protect key endurance sessions instead. A program cannot maximize every quality at the same time, but it can maintain one while emphasizing another.

Within a same-day session, doing resistance work before endurance tends to favor lower-body dynamic strength. Evidence is less decisive for hypertrophy and aerobic fitness. Session order is therefore a practical lever, not a magic switch. A tired, rushed strength session performed first can still be worse than a well-designed session performed later.

Separate demanding sessions when possible

When the schedule allows, separate hard endurance and heavy lower-body strength sessions by several hours or different days. This reduces acute fatigue and makes it easier to execute both sessions well. The exact ideal interval is not established for every population, and recovery depends on volume, intensity, nutrition, sleep, and training history.

Easy aerobic work usually creates less conflict than high-volume running or repeated hard intervals. Cycling may produce less muscle damage than running for some people, but modality should match goals and tolerance. Someone training for a running event still needs running; the answer is better dose control, not replacing all specific work.

Manage total stress, not just categories

Four moderate sessions may be easier to recover from than two heroic combined sessions. Count hard sets, long runs, intervals, sport practice, physically demanding work, and sleep disruption as parts of the same recovery budget. Interference can be confused with ordinary under-recovery when volume rises faster than capacity.

Warning signs include performance declining across several sessions, persistent soreness, loss of normal motivation, disturbed sleep, and an inability to complete previously manageable work. One bad workout is noise. A repeated downward pattern is a reason to reduce volume or intensity and reassess.

A practical weekly structure

A recreational trainee might use two full-body strength sessions, two easy aerobic sessions, and one optional harder endurance session. Avoid placing the hardest intervals immediately before the most important lower-body strength day. Keep at least one genuinely easy or rest day. Increase only one training stressor at a time.

  1. Choose a primary goal for the next four to eight weeks.
  2. Schedule its key sessions first.
  3. Add the secondary quality at the lowest effective dose.
  4. Separate hard lower-body sessions when practical.
  5. Track a few repeatable markers: load, repetitions, pace, heart rate, effort, and recovery.

What the research cannot promise

Meta-analyses combine programs that differ in exercises, duration, participants, and outcome measures. A group average cannot predict one person’s response. Short studies also cannot settle years-long programming questions. Sex-specific findings are limited by unequal representation, and “trained” means different things across studies.

The calm conclusion is that concurrent training works. It can improve strength and endurance together, especially for beginners and general-health goals. Interference becomes more relevant as training grows advanced, volumes rise, and goals become specific. Protect the priority, distribute fatigue, and adjust from measured response rather than fearing cardio as a category.
