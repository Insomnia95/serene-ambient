Niacin (Vitamin B3): What the Research Actually Shows

September 12, 2026 · 7 min read
Share

Niacin was identified in 1937 by Conrad Elvehjem as the cure for pellagra, a disease that killed roughly 100,000 Americans in the early 20th century and produced the "three Ds": dermatitis, diarrhea, and dementia. That history matters, because it explains why niacin is one of the few vitamins with genuinely dramatic deficiency disease behind it — and why the compound has since been pushed, at doses far above what prevents deficiency, as a treatment for high cholesterol. The two uses require understanding that "niacin" is not one thing.

Two Molecules, One Name

Vitamin B3 covers two distinct compounds. Nicotinic acid is the form used in the cholesterol trials and the one responsible for the notorious "niacin flush" — a hot, prickling, reddening sensation across the face and chest that starts within 15–30 minutes of dosing. Nicotinamide (niacinamide) is chemically different, does not cause flushing, and does not meaningfully affect blood lipids. Both correct a dietary deficiency equally well; they diverge entirely once you move into therapeutic-dose territory.

The Flush Reaction, Explained

The flush is caused by nicotinic acid binding a receptor (GPR109A) on skin immune cells, triggering a burst of prostaglandin D2 and subsequent vasodilation. It's uncomfortable but not dangerous, and tolerance builds within one to two weeks of consistent dosing. Manufacturers work around it in two ways: "flush-free" niacin, usually inositol hexanicotinate, which largely avoids the reaction but also appears to have weaker effects on lipids in the studies that have tested it; and flush-free double-strength formulations that still use bound nicotinic acid at a slower release rate. If your goal is deficiency correction or a general B-vitamin baseline rather than lipid management, the flush isn't a variable you need to negotiate with at all — niacinamide sidesteps it completely.

The Cardiovascular Trials: A Complicated Story

Niacin's reputation as a cholesterol drug comes from the Coronary Drug Project, a 1975 trial that found niacin reduced non-fatal heart attacks and, in long-term follow-up, reduced total mortality in men with prior heart attacks. For decades that result anchored niacin as a standard add-on lipid therapy, since it reliably raises HDL cholesterol and lowers triglycerides more than most other options available at the time.

Then came two large modern trials that complicated the picture substantially. AIM-HIGH, published in the New England Journal of Medicine in 2011, tested niacin added to statin therapy in patients with well-controlled LDL cholesterol and found no reduction in cardiovascular events despite the expected lipid improvements — and the trial was stopped early for futility. HPS2-THRIVE, published in the same journal in 2014, went further: extended-release niacin combined with a flushing-reduction drug produced no cardiovascular benefit and was associated with a higher rate of serious adverse events, including an unexpected increase in certain infections and, in diabetics, worse glycemic control. The takeaway most cardiologists now draw is that niacin's cholesterol-modifying effects don't reliably translate into fewer heart attacks once statins are already doing the heavy lifting.

Where It Still Makes Sense

Deficiency prevention remains uncontroversial — the RDA is 16mg NE for men and 14mg NE for women, easily met through diet (poultry, fish, peanuts, fortified grains) in most developed-world populations, which is why frank pellagra is now rare outside of alcoholism, malabsorption, or extremely restricted diets. Niacinamide has separate research support for skin conditions: a well-cited 2015 Australasian trial found oral nicotinamide reduced the rate of new non-melanoma skin cancers in high-risk patients, and topical niacinamide has decent evidence for acne and rosacea. Niacin is also the metabolic precursor to NAD+, the coenzyme central to cellular energy production, which is the mechanistic link researchers are exploring in the broader NAD-precursor longevity space — though that's a separate and much less settled conversation from cardiovascular dosing.

Dosing and Safety

For general nutritional support, doses at or near the RDA are sufficient and essentially risk-free. Therapeutic dosing for lipid management (1,000–2,000mg/day of nicotinic acid) is a different category entirely and carries real risks — hepatotoxicity, particularly with sustained-release formulations at high doses, elevated blood glucose, and gastrointestinal effects — and given the AIM-HIGH and HPS2-THRIVE results, most clinicians no longer recommend it as routine practice. That dose range should only be used under physician supervision with liver enzyme monitoring, not self-directed. The Tolerable Upper Intake Level set by the Institute of Medicine for flushing forms is 35mg/day for the general population specifically to avoid the flush and its downstream effects — a figure worth noting since it's far below what any lipid-management protocol would use.
