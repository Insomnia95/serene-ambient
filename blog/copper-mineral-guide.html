Copper: The Overlooked Mineral for Heart, Immunity, and Energy

August 29, 2026 · 7 min read
Share

Copper rarely gets discussed outside of niche nutrition circles, yet it's a cofactor in enzymes that build connective tissue, generate cellular energy, and regulate iron metabolism. The Linus Pauling Institute notes that copper deficiency is uncommon in the general population but disproportionately affects people on high-dose zinc supplementation, those who've had bariatric surgery, and anyone on long-term high-dose vitamin C or unsupervised zinc regimens — because zinc and copper compete for absorption at the same intestinal transporter.

What Copper Actually Does

Copper is a cofactor for cytochrome c oxidase, the final enzyme in the mitochondrial electron transport chain — meaning it's directly involved in how your cells produce ATP. It's also required for lysyl oxidase, an enzyme that cross-links collagen and elastin, which is why chronically low copper shows up as poor wound healing and connective tissue fragility. A third key enzyme, ceruloplasmin, depends on copper to oxidize iron so it can bind to transferrin and be transported through the blood — this is the main reason copper deficiency can produce an anemia that looks like iron deficiency but doesn't respond to iron alone.

Heart Health: The Emerging Picture

A review published in the journal Antioxidants (2022) laid out the case that defective copper metabolism contributes to several forms of heart disease, including cardiac hypertrophy, heart failure, and ischemic heart disease — noting that people with existing ischemic heart disease tend to show decreased cardiac copper content. The proposed mechanism runs through impaired mitochondrial energy production and reduced activity of copper-dependent antioxidant enzymes like superoxide dismutase, which normally helps clear the oxidative stress that damages heart tissue. This is an area of active research rather than settled science, but it's consistent with copper's known role in vascular collagen and elastin integrity — copper deficiency in animal models reliably produces vascular fragility and aneurysm formation.

Immune Function

Copper deficiency measurably impairs immune function. Documented effects include reduced proliferation of lymphocytes and impaired activity of both cytotoxic T cells and helper T cells; neutropenia — a drop in neutrophil count — is one of the more reliable clinical markers of copper deficiency in humans and is often what actually triggers a workup that ends in the diagnosis. A 2025 in-vitro study on copper bisglycinate specifically found immunomodulatory effects on cytokine signaling, supporting copper's role beyond a stopgap in nutrient tables. None of this means more copper equals a stronger immune system — deficiency correction restores normal function, but there's no good evidence that supplementing beyond sufficiency provides extra immune benefit.

Who's Actually at Risk

Outright dietary copper deficiency is rare if you eat a varied diet — organ meats, shellfish (especially oysters), nuts, seeds, and dark chocolate are all concentrated sources. The realistic risk groups are: people taking more than 40mg/day of zinc for extended periods (common in acne or immune-support protocols), anyone who's had gastric bypass or other bariatric procedures that reduce intestinal absorption surface, people with malabsorption conditions like celiac disease or Crohn's, and — less commonly — people on very high-dose vitamin C supplementation, which can also interfere with copper absorption. If you fall into one of these categories and you're dealing with unexplained fatigue, frequent infections, or an anemia that isn't responding to iron, copper status is worth asking your doctor to check.

Dosing and Form

The RDA for adults is 900 micrograms per day — a genuinely small amount, which is part of why supplementing without a specific reason isn't necessary for most people. Chelated forms like copper bisglycinate are absorbed more efficiently and cause less GI irritation than copper sulfate or copper oxide. The tolerable upper intake level is set at 10,000 micrograms (10mg) per day for adults — chronic intake above this range is associated with liver toxicity, since the liver is the primary site of copper storage and excretion via bile.

If you supplement zinc long-term for any reason, a general rule used in clinical nutrition is a zinc-to-copper ratio no higher than roughly 10:1 — most multivitamins and dedicated copper glycinate products are formulated with this ratio in mind, or sold standalone so you can dose precisely against whatever zinc protocol you're already running.

Bottom Line

Copper isn't a mineral most people need to actively supplement — dietary sources cover the RDA comfortably for the majority of the population. It becomes relevant when you're on long-term high-dose zinc, have had bariatric surgery, or have a malabsorption condition. In those cases, correcting a real deficiency has clear, well-documented effects on energy metabolism, connective tissue integrity, and immune cell function. Outside of those situations, more copper is not a performance lever — it's a nutrient you want to be sufficient in, not maximized.
