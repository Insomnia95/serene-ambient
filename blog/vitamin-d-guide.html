Vitamin D: The Deficiency Most People Don't Know They Have

May 25, 2026 · 7 min read
Share

Vitamin D occupies an unusual category in nutrition science: it's technically a vitamin but functions as a steroid hormone, influencing gene expression in virtually every tissue in the body. Receptors for vitamin D have been found in over 35 different tissues, including the brain, immune cells, heart, and gut. This isn't a vitamin involved in one pathway — it's a systemic regulator.

The problem: over 40 percent of American adults have blood levels below the threshold most researchers now consider optimal. The cause is partly modern indoor lifestyles, partly sunscreen use, and partly the fact that the official RDA (600–800 IU) was calculated decades ago using a methodology that most vitamin D researchers now believe produced a number that's 8 to 10 times too low.

Sun vs. Supplementation

Your skin produces vitamin D3 when UVB rays hit it — at latitudes above approximately 35 degrees north (which includes most of the continental US), this synthesis is essentially zero from October through March, regardless of how much time you spend outside. Even in summer, glass blocks UVB entirely, and sunscreen at SPF 30 reduces synthesis by about 95 percent.

The practical reality for most people in northern climates: supplementation is not optional, it's the only viable path to adequate levels for roughly half the year.

What Optimal Actually Means

The official "sufficient" threshold is 20 ng/mL (50 nmol/L). But an increasing body of research suggests that the health benefits — immune function, mood regulation, cancer risk reduction, bone density, cardiovascular markers — continue to improve up to approximately 50–60 ng/mL, and that below 30 ng/mL represents functional insufficiency even if it isn't technically "deficiency."

A meta-analysis of 25 randomized trials published in the BMJ found that vitamin D supplementation reduced respiratory tract infections by 12 percent overall, and by 70 percent in participants with baseline deficiency. The immune effect is real and large — particularly below sufficient thresholds.

Dosing and Testing

Researchers like Dr. Michael Holick (whose work on vitamin D spans four decades) suggest that most adults require 1500–2000 IU daily to maintain levels above 30 ng/mL, and 3000–5000 IU to maintain 40–60 ng/mL. The tolerable upper limit is set at 4000 IU, but toxicity typically requires sustained dosing above 10,000 IU for extended periods.

The single most useful thing you can do is get tested. A 25-hydroxyvitamin D blood test (25(OH)D) costs under $50 and tells you your actual status. Supplementation without testing means you may be under-dosing (most common) or theoretically over-dosing. Most people test once in late autumn to establish a baseline. Cofactors

Vitamin D requires magnesium for activation — which creates a common bottleneck for people deficient in both. It also works synergistically with vitamin K2 (MK-7 form) for calcium regulation: D3 increases calcium absorption, K2 directs that calcium to bones rather than arteries.
