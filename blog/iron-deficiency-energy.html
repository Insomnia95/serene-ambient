Iron Deficiency and Fatigue: What the Research Says About Restoring Energy

June 28, 2026 · 8 min read
Share

Iron deficiency is the world's most common nutritional deficiency, affecting an estimated two billion people globally according to the World Health Organization. Yet it remains chronically underdiagnosed, partly because its primary symptom — fatigue — is so non-specific that it's easily attributed to stress, poor sleep, or simply being busy. The result is that a significant number of people function for months or years below their potential because of a deficiency that is both detectable and correctable.

What Iron Actually Does

Iron's central role in the body is oxygen transport. It is a core component of hemoglobin, the protein in red blood cells that binds oxygen in the lungs and releases it in peripheral tissues. Without adequate iron, red blood cell production falls, oxygen delivery to muscles and organs declines, and the result is the classic symptom cluster: persistent fatigue, reduced exercise tolerance, difficulty concentrating, and in more severe cases, shortness of breath and pallor.

Iron is also required for the synthesis of dopamine, serotonin, and norepinephrine — neurotransmitters governing mood and cognitive function. A 2007 study in the European Journal of Clinical Nutrition found that non-anemic iron-deficient women showed significant impairments in attention and memory, independent of their hemoglobin levels. Brain symptoms can precede anemia by months.

Who Is at Risk

Premenopausal women are the highest-risk group due to menstrual blood loss. Athletes — particularly female endurance athletes — face compounded risk from blood loss, foot-strike hemolysis (mechanical destruction of red blood cells), and elevated iron demand from high training loads. Vegetarians and vegans are also at elevated risk: plant-based iron (non-heme iron) has substantially lower bioavailability than heme iron from animal sources, and plant foods often contain phytates and polyphenols that further inhibit absorption.

Growing adolescents, pregnant women, and frequent blood donors round out the at-risk population. Even among men, gastrointestinal blood loss from conditions like ulcers or frequent NSAID use can deplete iron stores silently.

Testing: What to Ask For

A standard complete blood count (CBC) will flag anemia — low hemoglobin — but it misses iron deficiency without anemia, which is the earlier and more prevalent stage. The relevant markers are serum ferritin (the body's iron storage protein) and serum iron with transferrin saturation. Ferritin below 30 ng/mL is associated with fatigue symptoms even in the absence of anemia; optimal levels for most people are 50–100 ng/mL.

Many standard blood panels don't include ferritin by default. Ask specifically for a "full iron panel" or "iron studies" including serum ferritin when discussing fatigue with a physician.

Supplementation: Form Matters

Ferrous sulfate — the cheapest and most commonly prescribed iron supplement — is effective but causes gastrointestinal side effects (nausea, constipation, stomach pain) in a significant proportion of users. The side effects are dose-dependent and related to unabsorbed iron irritating the intestinal lining.

Iron bisglycinate, a chelated form where iron is bound to two glycine molecules, has similar or superior absorption with substantially fewer gastrointestinal effects. A 2014 study in the Journal of Nutrition found iron bisglycinate to be 2.5 times more bioavailable than ferrous sulfate in iron-depleted women. Vitamin C and Absorption

Vitamin C (ascorbic acid) dramatically enhances non-heme iron absorption by reducing ferric iron (Fe³⁺) to the more absorbable ferrous form (Fe²⁺) and by forming a chelate that remains soluble in the intestinal environment. A meta-analysis published in the American Journal of Clinical Nutrition found that concurrent vitamin C increased non-heme iron absorption by 67 percent on average. Taking vitamin C alongside an iron supplement — or alongside iron-rich plant foods — is one of the most evidence-supported nutritional synergies available.

Conversely, calcium, tannins (in tea and coffee), and phytates all inhibit iron absorption. Taking iron with coffee or tea can reduce absorption by up to 60 percent. Spacing iron supplementation from these inhibitors by at least an hour is worth the effort.

Dietary Sources and the Heme Difference

Heme iron — found in red meat, poultry, and seafood — is absorbed at 15–35 percent efficiency regardless of other dietary factors. Non-heme iron — found in legumes, dark leafy greens, tofu, and fortified grains — absorbs at 2–20 percent, highly dependent on what else is consumed simultaneously. Red meat, liver, clams, and oysters are the highest-concentration heme sources. Among plant sources, lentils, spinach, and pumpkin seeds provide the most iron per serving, but the absorption ceiling is lower.

Cooking in a cast iron skillet measurably increases the iron content of acidic foods — a 1986 study in the Journal of the American Dietetic Association found that cooking tomato sauce in cast iron for 20 minutes increased iron content from 0.61 to 5.77mg per 100g. A practical, passive way to add dietary iron for those who cook frequently.

Timeline for Recovery

Hemoglobin typically begins recovering within 4–8 weeks of adequate supplementation. Ferritin stores, which were depleted first, take considerably longer — often 3–6 months of consistent supplementation to fully restore. Fatigue often improves before blood markers fully normalize, as tissues respond to improved oxygen delivery before lab values catch up. Complete repletion requires sustained supplementation well beyond the point of symptom resolution.
