Stoicism and CBT: The Philosophical Roots of Modern Psychology

July 18, 2026 · 8 min read
Share

In 1955, psychologist Albert Ellis founded what became Rational Emotive Behavior Therapy (REBT), the direct forerunner of modern cognitive behavioral therapy. He didn't hide his source material. Ellis repeatedly cited a single line from the Stoic philosopher Epictetus's Enchiridion as the founding insight of his entire clinical model: "Men are disturbed not by things, but by the views which they take of them." That sentence, written around 125 CE by a former slave turned philosophy teacher, is functionally identical to the cognitive model that underlies CBT today.

A Direct, Acknowledged Lineage

This isn't a loose modern analogy retrofitted onto ancient texts. Ellis stated explicitly in his writing that Epictetus's framing gave him the core structural idea for REBT — that emotional disturbance follows from beliefs about events, not from events themselves. Aaron Beck, who developed cognitive therapy independently in the 1960s while working with depressed patients at the University of Pennsylvania, also acknowledged Stoic and Epicurean philosophy as intellectual ancestors of the cognitive model, particularly the idea that distorted automatic thoughts, not circumstances, generate emotional distress.

Both therapies rest on what's now called the ABC model: an Activating event, a Belief about that event, and a Consequence in emotion and behavior. Strip the modern clinical vocabulary away and the structure is Epictetus's dichotomy of control restated in three letters — external events are not in your power, your judgments about them are, and psychological suffering tracks the judgments, not the events.

Cognitive Distancing: Same Technique, Two Names

CBT's central technique is cognitive restructuring — noticing an automatic thought, treating it as a hypothesis rather than a fact, and examining the evidence for and against it. Marcus Aurelius practiced a nearly identical exercise in his private notebook, later published as Meditations, repeatedly instructing himself to strip an event down to its bare factual description before adding any judgment to it — a technique the historian and cognitive therapist Donald Robertson has termed "cognitive distancing" in his comparative writing on Stoicism and CBT.

The Stoics also anticipated exposure-based techniques. Seneca advised imagining the worst-case outcome in vivid detail in advance — premeditatio malorum — a practice that maps closely onto the imaginal exposure and decatastrophizing exercises used in modern CBT for anxiety disorders. The goal in both cases is identical: reduce the emotional charge of a feared outcome by confronting it deliberately, under controlled conditions, rather than avoiding it.

What the Modern Evidence Shows

CBT itself has one of the largest evidence bases in clinical psychology. A 2012 meta-analysis by Stefan Hofmann and colleagues in Cognitive Therapy and Research, reviewing 269 studies, found consistent, large effect sizes for CBT across anxiety disorders, and moderate-to-large effects for depression — among the most robust outcomes in the psychotherapy literature.

The Stoic-specific practices have their own, smaller but growing evidence base. The University of Exeter's "Stoic Week" project, led by researcher Tim LeBon and colleagues, has run annual trials since 2013 in which thousands of participants practice Stoic exercises — the dichotomy of control, negative visualization, evening review — for seven days. Published results consistently show statistically significant increases in life satisfaction and decreases in negative affect over the week, with effect sizes comparable to short structured CBT-based interventions, though the study design (self-selected participants, no long-term follow-up) is weaker than a randomized clinical trial.

Where the Frameworks Diverge

CBT is a manualized clinical treatment, developed and tested against specific diagnostic outcomes, delivered by trained clinicians, and refined for over 60 years using randomized controlled trials. Stoicism is a 2,300-year-old ethical philosophy concerned with living well as a whole, not treating a specific disorder. Using Epictetus's Enchiridion as a substitute for evidence-based treatment of clinical depression or panic disorder is not what the research supports — Ellis and Beck extracted a specific insight from Stoicism and built a testable, falsifiable clinical model around it, which is a different thing than the philosophy itself being clinical treatment.

What the Stoic texts offer well is a daily practice layer that sits comfortably alongside CBT skills — reframing techniques you can rehearse in a journal each morning and evening, independent of a therapy appointment. That's largely why cognitive-behavioral clinicians like Robertson have written books translating Stoic exercises directly into structured, CBT-compatible formats such as A Guide to the Good Life.

Reading Both Traditions

For the clinical, symptom-focused side, Beck's cognitive model is best encountered through David Burns's Feeling Good, which popularized Beck's framework into a self-administered CBT format credited in outcome studies with producing measurable symptom reduction comparable to some medication trials for mild-to-moderate depression. For the source material Ellis himself pointed to, Seneca's letters — collected in Letters from a Stoic — read less like ancient philosophy and more like a correspondence course in exactly the reframing techniques a CBT therapist would recognize today.
