Exercised by Daniel Lieberman: An Evidence-Based Review

November 5, 2026 · 9 min read
Share XFacebookPinterest

Most exercise books begin by telling readers to move more. Daniel Lieberman begins with a more interesting question: if exercise is so beneficial, why do many people avoid it whenever they can?

His answer is that exercise—planned, discretionary physical activity for health or fitness—is culturally recent. Humans evolved to spend energy when movement was necessary or rewarding, and to conserve it when rest was available. Laziness, in this account, is not a moral defect. It is part of an energy-budgeting system placed in an environment that no longer requires much movement.

The book’s strongest move: remove the moral drama

Exercised is most useful when it replaces blame with design. If voluntary workouts compete with comfort, work, childcare, and immediate rewards, repeating “be disciplined” explains little. Lieberman instead asks how cultures make movement necessary, social, enjoyable, or meaningful.

This frame fits behavioral evidence better than the idea that knowledge alone changes habits. People generally know that activity is healthy. The gap is often opportunity, pain, safety, time, social support, or the absence of an immediate reason to move. The book makes those barriers feel predictable rather than shameful.

Movement is not the same as training

Lieberman draws on studies of hunter-gatherer and subsistence populations to challenge simple myths. People in such groups do not necessarily spend every waking hour in motion. They rest, sit, and avoid needless effort. Yet their days often include more walking, carrying, squatting, climbing, and variable physical work than industrialized life requires.

The distinction matters. A long gym session cannot make every other hour irrelevant, but neither is constant low-level movement a substitute for the strength, balance, or aerobic stimulus some bodies need. The practical lesson is a portfolio: frequent ordinary movement, some harder cardiovascular work, and resistance exercise appropriate to age and ability.

The evolutionary argument is a lens, not a prescription

Evolutionary mismatch can explain why a trait that once saved energy may create problems in an environment of abundant calories, chairs, cars, and screens. It cannot tell an individual exactly how many minutes to run or which exercise is medically safe. An ancestral pattern is not automatically optimal, and past populations differed enormously in ecology, workload, diet, lifespan, and disease exposure.

Lieberman usually acknowledges this complexity, but the storytelling force of the book can make observational comparisons feel more decisive than they are. Modern exercise guidelines come from epidemiology and intervention research, not from reconstructing one ideal ancestral lifestyle. The evolutionary story helps explain motivation; it does not replace clinical evidence.

What the book gets right about sitting

Exercised resists a popular exaggeration: that sitting is inherently unnatural. Humans have always rested. The problem is prolonged, uninterrupted inactivity combined with too little total movement and poor cardiometabolic context. This is a more useful distinction than treating every chair as toxic.

For readers, the actionable version is modest: interrupt long periods of sitting, walk when a trip can reasonably be walked, and build activity into routines rather than relying only on heroic workouts. These changes reduce the number of decisions required.

Aging is where the argument becomes urgent

The book’s sections on aging emphasize that exercise is not a fountain of youth. It is a signal that helps maintain capacities the body otherwise has little reason to keep. Strength, aerobic fitness, balance, and bone loading become especially relevant as reserve declines.

That does not mean prescribing the same training to everyone. Frailty, arthritis, cardiovascular disease, disability, and recent illness change the starting point. The principle is scalable stress followed by recovery, not punishment. A supervised chair rise can be training; so can a loaded carry or a hill interval. Context determines the dose.

Where readers should be cautious

First, the book ranges across anthropology, biomechanics, epidemiology, and public health. No single narrative can treat every controversy in depth. Second, broad statements about “humans” can understate sex, disability, occupation, income, environment, and cultural differences. Third, evidence that active people have better outcomes is partly vulnerable to reverse causation: healthier people can do more.

Lieberman does not ignore these limits, but readers may remember the vivid field stories more readily than the caveats. Use the book to generate better questions—why is movement absent, what would make it meaningful, which capacity matters now—rather than to copy another population’s life.

Who should read it

This is an excellent book for someone who understands that exercise matters but resents the moral language surrounding it. Coaches and health communicators will also find a better vocabulary for discussing inactivity without contempt. Readers seeking a precise program will need another resource; Exercised explains the problem more fully than it prescribes a routine.

The practical takeaway

  1. Stop treating reluctance to exercise as proof of bad character.
  2. Make movement serve an immediate purpose: transport, play, company, skill, or time outdoors.
  3. Reduce friction by placing short activity inside an existing routine.
  4. Train more than one capacity: endurance alone does not preserve strength or balance.
  5. Progress from your real baseline, not an imagined ancestral one.

The book’s central contribution is not a novel workout. It is a humane explanation for why exercise can feel unnecessary even when it is beneficial. Once blame is removed, the practical task becomes clearer: design a life in which movement is easier to choose, more rewarding now, and appropriately challenging over time.
