Vitamin B2 (Riboflavin): Benefits, Deficiency Signs & Dosage

September 25, 2026 · 7 min read
Share

Riboflavin, known as vitamin B2, is the precursor for two coenzymes — FAD and FMN — that sit at the center of nearly every energy-producing reaction inside the human cell. It's water-soluble and abundant in ordinary foods like eggs, dairy, and leafy greens, so outright deficiency is uncommon in people eating a varied diet. The more interesting development in the research has little to do with deficiency: high-dose riboflavin has become one of the better-studied non-drug options for migraine prevention, with effect sizes that hold up across randomized trials. Here is what the evidence actually supports on both fronts, and how to dose it correctly.

What Riboflavin Actually Does

As FAD (flavin adenine dinucleotide) and FMN (flavin mononucleotide), riboflavin acts as an obligatory cofactor for enzymes in the mitochondrial electron transport chain — specifically Complex I and Complex II — meaning a real riboflavin shortfall can measurably reduce how efficiently cells generate ATP. FAD is also required to regenerate glutathione, the body's primary intracellular antioxidant, through the enzyme glutathione reductase. This dual role in energy metabolism and antioxidant recycling is why deficiency symptoms overlap so heavily with generalized fatigue, and why researchers have looked at riboflavin specifically for conditions involving mitochondrial stress — migraine chief among them.

Migraine Prevention: The Strongest Evidence

The clearest riboflavin research is a 1998 randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial by Schoenen and colleagues, published in Neurology, which gave 55 migraine patients either 400mg of riboflavin or a placebo daily for three months. 59 percent of the riboflavin group had at least a 50 percent reduction in migraine frequency, compared with 15 percent on placebo — one of the larger effect sizes reported for any non-drug migraine intervention at the time. Subsequent reviews, including the Canadian Headache Society's 2012 prophylactic guidelines, list riboflavin as a Level B ("probably effective") option, in the same tier as magnesium and coenzyme Q10. A 2009 study by Condò and colleagues in The Journal of Headache and Pain found that 200mg daily reduced migraine frequency in children and adolescents by roughly 50 percent over three months, suggesting the mechanism isn't specific to adult physiology. Deficiency: Who's Actually At Risk

Classic riboflavin deficiency, or ariboflavinosis, produces angular stomatitis (cracking at the corners of the mouth), cheilosis, a magenta-colored tongue, and seborrheic dermatitis around the nose and ears. It's also linked to a specific type of anemia that doesn't correct with iron alone, since FAD is required by the enzyme that mobilizes iron for hemoglobin synthesis. True deficiency is rare with a varied diet, but the higher-risk groups are people with chronic alcohol use disorder, since alcohol impairs riboflavin absorption and storage, long-term users of oral contraceptives and tricyclic antidepressants, which interfere with riboflavin metabolism, strict vegans who don't eat fortified foods, and endurance athletes, whose riboflavin turnover rises with training volume based on urinary excretion studies from exercise physiology research.

Dosing and Forms

The RDA for adults is 1.1mg (women) to 1.3mg (men) daily — trivially easy to hit through diet alone. Migraine-prevention protocols use the much higher 400mg daily dose, taken as a single amount or split, for a minimum of three months before assessing effect, since the Schoenen trial and its replications found the benefit builds gradually rather than immediately. Riboflavin also comes as riboflavin-5'-phosphate (R5P), the pre-activated coenzyme form. Thorne's Riboflavin 5'-Phosphate skips a conversion step the liver otherwise performs, which may matter for people with reduced flavin-converting enzyme activity, though for most healthy adults standard riboflavin converts efficiently enough that the practical difference is small. A lower-cost R5P option like Swanson's R-5-P covers the same bioactive form at a lower dose per capsule. One reliable sign riboflavin is being absorbed and excreted as expected: urine turns bright yellow within hours of a high dose — a harmless, well-documented effect, not a sign of toxicity. Riboflavin has no established upper intake limit because unused amounts are efficiently cleared by the kidneys.

Quality Indicators

Because 400mg sits well above the RDA, single-ingredient products are more practical than multivitamins for migraine-dose protocols — reaching that dose through a multivitamin would require an impractical pill count and would also deliver megadoses of nutrients you don't need. Look for third-party testing (USP, NSF, or Informed Choice) and avoid products that bury riboflavin inside a proprietary "energy blend" without listing an isolated milligram amount.
